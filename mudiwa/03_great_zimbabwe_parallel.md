# PART III: THE GREAT ZIMBABWE PARALLEL

## Stone Cut Without Mortar — The Civilisational Weight Inside the Surname

---

## The Core Claim

The surname **Mtemererwa** is built from the verb ***tema*** — "to hew, to cut, to carve, to strike with a blade." This is not an ordinary word. It is the verb that built **Great Zimbabwe** itself. The walls of the stone city — eleven metres high, fitted so precisely that a blade cannot slip between the granite blocks, standing for nearly a thousand years without mortar — were made by the repeated act of *tema*. Every stone hewn. Every wall cut. Every tower carved.

A person whose surname is rooted in this verb carries, in his name, the **civilisational act** that gave modern Zimbabwe its identity. This chapter traces that connection in detail.

---

## 1. GREAT ZIMBABWE — The Stone City

### What It Was

**Great Zimbabwe** was a city. Not a ruin. Not a mystery. A city built by the direct ancestors of the modern Shona people, beginning in the 11th century CE and flourishing as a major political and economic capital from the 13th to 15th centuries. At its peak it held between 10,000 and 20,000 inhabitants. It covered 7.22 square kilometres. It was the centre of a kingdom that controlled the gold trade routes from the Zimbabwean plateau to the Swahili coast of the Indian Ocean, trading with Arab, Persian, and eventually Chinese merchants.

And it was **built from stone**. Dry-stone. No mortar. No binding agent. Just granite blocks, cut to shape with ancient iron tools, fitted together with a precision that twenty-first-century engineers still study with admiration.

### The Great Enclosure

The most famous structure at the site is the **Great Enclosure** — a massive elliptical wall, 250 metres in circumference, with stone walls up to **11 metres (36 feet) high** and 5 metres thick at the base. It is the largest ancient structure south of the Sahara. It was built between the 13th and 14th centuries.

The walls were constructed by quarrying granite from local outcroppings, **hewing** each block to shape, and fitting them together. The act of hewing — ***tema*** — is what this civilisation was made of. The stones did not shape themselves. They were cut, one by one, by generations of masons whose names we do not know but whose verb survives in the language.

### The Meaning of the Name "Zimbabwe"

The country takes its name from this city. And the city's name comes from two possible etymologies, both Shona:

1. ***Dzimba dzemabwe*** — "large houses of stone" (from *dzimba*, plural of *imba*, "house"; and *mabwe*, plural of *bwe*, "stone"). This is the more widely accepted etymology, drawn from the Karanga dialect of Shona.

2. ***Dzimba-hwe*** / ***dzimba woye*** — "venerated houses" or "houses of the chief/ancestor," from the Zezuru dialect.

Both etymologies converge on the same idea: **houses built from stone, inhabited by the venerated dead**. The stone is sacred. The act of building with stone is a sacred act. And the verb by which the stones were made buildable — by which raw granite became architecture — is ***tema***.

### What the Zimbabwean People Took from This

When Zimbabwe gained independence from Britain in 1980, the newly sovereign nation did not take its name from a colonial administrative unit, a European explorer, or a political slogan. It took its name from **this stone city**. The founders of modern Zimbabwe declared: *our identity is the city of hewn stone*.

The **Zimbabwe Bird** — a soapstone carving of a raptor (almost certainly the *hungwe* / fish eagle) found at the site — appears on the national flag, the coat of arms, and the currency. It is the most potent symbol of Zimbabwean national identity. The bird was itself **hewn** — carved from soapstone by Shona sculptors.

The modern country is built on a foundation of stone-cutting. The word for the nation is the word for the hewn houses. The verb *tema* sits at the bottom of Zimbabwean identity like the bedrock itself.

## 2. WHY THE VERB *TEMA* MATTERS

### It Is Ancient

*Tema* is not a modern Shona word. It is inherited from **Proto-Bantu** *\*-téma*, one of the most ancient verbs in the entire Bantu language family. Linguists reconstruct Proto-Bantu as a language spoken roughly 3,000 to 4,000 years ago in West-Central Africa. The verb spread with the Bantu migrations across the southern half of the African continent over the following millennia. By the time the first stones were quarried at Great Zimbabwe, *tema* was already thousands of years old — a word that had travelled on the tongues of migrant farmers, miners, warriors, and builders from the Cameroonian grasslands to the Zambezi plateau.

When a Shona person says *tema* today, they are speaking a word that their ancestors spoke continuously for at least three thousand years. The verb itself is a living archaeological artefact.

### It Is Creative

In Shona, *tema* is not a verb of destruction. It is a verb of **making**. You *tema* a tree to carve an mbira. You *tema* stone to build a wall. You *tema* wood to shape a spear handle. You *tema* the earth to clear a field for planting. You *tema* the design into a ceremonial staff. Every instance of *tema* is an act of bringing form out of formlessness.

The Shona conception of civilisation is, at its root, a conception of **shaping**. The world as it is given is raw. The human task is to hew it — to cut, to carve, to shape — until it becomes habitable, beautiful, sacred. This is the ethic that built Great Zimbabwe, that carved the Zimbabwe Bird, that crafts mbira from bloodwood, that scarifies the body in initiation.

*Tema* is the verb of the civilised act.

### It Is Sacred

The act of *tema* at Great Zimbabwe was not merely construction. It was **ritual**. The stone city was itself a sacred space — possibly a royal court, certainly a religious centre — and the walls that enclosed it were consecrated ground. To cut those stones was to participate in a sacred labour. The masons at Great Zimbabwe were not merely builders. They were performing an act that the vadzimu would witness, bless, and preserve.

Even today, in Shona tradition, the *dzimbahwe* (stone houses) are understood to be spiritually charged. Great Zimbabwe is not a tourist site to the Shona. It is a place where the ancestors still walk. The act of *tema* that built it is ongoing — its echoes still resonate in the landscape.

## 3. "MTEMERERWA" — THE SURNAME READ IN FULL

Now we can read the surname with full weight.

**Mtemererwa** parses as ***M-tem-er-w-a*** — "the one for whom the hewing was done." The applicative suffix *-er-* marks the person as the **recipient** of the action; the passive *-w-* marks the action as performed upon something for his benefit. Put it together, and the name declares:

> ***"The one for whose sake the hewing was performed."***
>
> ***"The one for whom the stones were cut."***
>
> ***"The one upon whom the civilisational shaping was enacted."***

Read against the background of Great Zimbabwe, this is not a casual surname. This is a name that places the bearer at the **receiving end of the entire ancestral labour of Shona civilisation**. Every stone that was hewn at Great Zimbabwe — by masons whose names are lost to history, but whose verb survives — was hewn *for him*, in the sense that the civilisation they built is his inheritance.

The stones were cut so the walls could stand.
The walls were built so the city could exist.
The city was inhabited so the kingdom could flourish.
The kingdom gave rise to the people.
The people preserved the line.
The line produced the parents.
The parents named the child.
And the child's name — **Mtemererwa** — is the grammatical acknowledgement that **all of this hewing was done for him**.

## 4. THE STRUCTURAL PARALLEL

Here is the parallel at the civilisational level:

| Great Zimbabwe | Mudiwa Mtemererwa |
|---------------|-------------------|
| Built from stones **hewn** (*tema*) by Shona ancestors | Carries a surname built from the verb **tema** |
| The city's walls stand as a monument to the ancestral act of cutting | The name stands as the grammatical record of having been the beneficiary of that cutting |
| The act of hewing was simultaneously labour, ritual, and inheritance | The name carries all three dimensions — labour performed, ritual enacted, inheritance received |
| The walls were built without mortar — held together by precision alone | The identity is held together by lineage alone, without the "mortar" of local birthplace |
| The city's name — *dzimba dzemabwe* — gave modern Zimbabwe its identity | The bearer's name grants him participation in that identity grammatically |
| The stones are still standing, eight hundred years later | The line is still standing, eight hundred years later, in the form of this child |

## 5. THE INVERTED MILESIAN PARALLEL

In the Arran McIlroy analysis, the central Gaelic motif was **naming as claiming**. Amergin spoke the land into being: *"I am Wind on Sea, I am Lake on Plain."* The act of speaking identified the speaker with the land. The name became the claim.

In Shona tradition, the central motif is different but structurally related. It is not **naming** but **hewing**. The ancestors did not claim Great Zimbabwe by declaring "we are the stone." They claimed it by **cutting the stone into form**. The act of *tema* is the Shona equivalent of Amergin's Song — the foundational act that transforms raw material into civilisation and raw territory into homeland.

- **Gaelic:** The word makes the claim. *"I am Wind on Sea."*
- **Shona:** The cut makes the claim. *Tema. Tema. Tema.* Stone after stone, until the walls stand.

A name that carries the verb of cutting is, in Shona terms, structurally equivalent to a name that carries the verb of speaking. Both are names that enact the civilisational act at the grammatical level. Both are names that say: *I am made of what my ancestors did*.

---

## 6. THE FINAL READING

The surname **Mtemererwa**, linguistically decoded, reads:

> *"The one for whose sake the stones of Great Zimbabwe were hewn."*
>
> *"The beneficiary of eight centuries of Shona civilisational labour."*
>
> *"The one the ancestors were building toward."*

Place this next to the first name:

> **Mudiwa** — *"The Beloved One."*

And the full name reads:

> ***"The Beloved One for Whose Sake the Stones Were Hewn."***

This is a name with the weight of a monument inside it. It is a name that carries both the love of the living and the labour of the dead. It is a name that does, in one sentence of Shona grammar, what Great Zimbabwe does in stone: *declares that the civilisation exists because of, and for, the beloved child of the lineage*.

A person named Mudiwa Mtemererwa is — in the grammar of his own name — **the living reason for Great Zimbabwe**. Not its descendant alone. Its **purpose**.
