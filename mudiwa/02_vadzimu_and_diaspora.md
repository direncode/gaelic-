# PART II: THE VADZIMU AND THE DIASPORA

## The Ancestors Travel With Their Children — A Birmingham Birth in Shona Cosmology

---

## The Central Question

What does it mean, in Shona tradition, for a Zimbabwean child to be born in Birmingham, England — 8,000 kilometres from the ancestral homeland?

The question has a specific, ancient, and unambiguous answer in Shona thought. And that answer has nothing to do with exile, loss, or disconnection.

---

## 1. THE VADZIMU — The Living Dead

In Shona cosmology, the dead are not gone. They are **vadzimu** — ancestral spirits — and they are active participants in the lives of their descendants. The vadzimu belief "is at the core of indigenous Shona religion" (Tarusarira et al., *Divine Angels and Vadzimu in Shona Religion*).

The structure is precise:

- **Mwari** — the supreme creator, the maker of humankind (*Musikavanhu*), the one above (*Wokumusoro*). Distant, all-powerful, never approached directly.
- **Vadzimu** — the ancestors of a specific family line. They are the **intermediaries** between Mwari and the living. One does not pray to Mwari. One asks the vadzimu, and the vadzimu carry the request upward.
- **The living descendants** — the current generation, who maintain the relationship with the vadzimu through prayer, libation, ceremony, and — most dramatically — through the *svikiro* (spirit medium) during a *bira* ceremony.

This is not folklore or metaphor. It is the **operative religious cosmology** of traditional Shona life, still practised today in both Zimbabwe and the diaspora.

### How the Vadzimu Are Made

A person does not automatically become a *mudzimu* (ancestor) at death. There is a ritual transformation. When a Shona person dies, their spirit is understood to wander — restless, liminal, unplaced — until the **kurova guva** ceremony is performed. The name means "beating the grave." Typically held one year after death, the ritual formally invites the spirit home and incorporates it into the ancestral line.

After kurova guva, the deceased becomes a **protective mudzimu** — watching over children, grandchildren, great-grandchildren, and all descendants to come. From that moment onward, the ancestor is not absent. The ancestor is **present** — attending births, watching marriages, listening at meals, answering prayers, warning in dreams.

---

## 2. THE DIASPORA PRINCIPLE — They Travel With Us

Here is the passage that matters most for anyone asking what it means to be a Shona child born in Britain. It comes directly from the traditional Shona religious tradition, as recorded in multiple scholarly and community sources:

> ***"The work of the vadzimu is to protect their descendants. They are more interested in their own children than anyone else. They guard their people wherever they may be. Nowadays we find members of a household scattered everywhere, some may be studying in America, England and India, but the vadzimu still see these people and protect them."***

Read that again. Slowly.

**The vadzimu still see these people and protect them.**

This is not a modern accommodation to diaspora conditions. It is the traditional theology stated in traditional terms. The ancestors do not stay at the *musha* (ancestral homestead). They do not remain in Zimbabwe while their descendants emigrate. They **travel**. They follow. They are present in London, in Johannesburg, in Toronto, in Birmingham — wherever a descendant of their lineage walks, they walk beside.

### The Implication for a Birmingham Birth

A Shona child born in Birmingham to Zimbabwean parents is not an exile. Not in Shona terms. The child is not separated from the ancestors by distance, because distance does not separate the vadzimu from their descendants. The moment the child was conceived, the full weight of the ancestral line was already present — watching, protecting, claiming.

The child's *mutupo* (clan totem) is inherited **patrilineally, regardless of birthplace**. A child born in Birmingham to a Shona father of the Soko clan is Soko. A child born to a Shava (eland) clan father is Shava. There is no dilution, no attenuation, no loss. Totem and ancestral protection are transmitted through blood, not soil.

This is structurally identical to the principle at work in the Gaelic *dúchas* — the concept that heritage is carried in the lineage, not the landscape. But where Gaelic *dúchas* is abstract and conceptual, Shona *vadzimu* is **personal and populated**. The Gaels carry their heritage. The Shona carry their ancestors.

---

## 3. THE NAME MUDIWA IN THIS COSMOLOGY

Now apply this cosmology to the name **Mudiwa** — "the beloved one."

A Shona child is not beloved only by their living parents. In the operative cosmology, the child is beloved by:

- The mother and father
- The grandparents (if living)
- The *vadzimu* — every ancestor in the patrilineal line, stretching back generations beyond counting
- Eventually, through the ancestors, **Mwari** himself — who receives the love directed upward through the chain of intercession

When Zimbabwean parents in Birmingham named their child *Mudiwa*, they were not only saying *we love this child*. They were saying *this child is beloved by the entire line of the dead who walk with us, who followed us across the ocean, who are present in this hospital room, who watch this birth, who claim this child as one of their own*.

A name like Mudiwa, given in the diaspora, is a **declaration of continuity**. It says: the ancestors are still here. They still love. The line did not break when we crossed the sea. The beloved child is beloved by the full lineage — and the full lineage came with us.

---

## 4. BIRMINGHAM, THE DIASPORA CITY

Birmingham has one of the largest Zimbabwean communities in the United Kingdom. Scholarly work (McGregor and Primorac, *Zimbabwean Communities in Britain*, Palgrave 2018) documents the community's history, its Shona-speaking majority, its religious institutions (including the Zimbabwe Catholic Community in Birmingham), and its intricate mechanisms for cultural preservation.

The community did not arrive in Britain as individuals. It arrived as families, lineages, clans — carrying their *mitupo* (totems), their praise poetry (*nhetembo*), their mbira traditions, their ceremonies. Birmingham became, and remains, one of the significant nodes of the Shona diaspora. The ancestors came with them. Shona funeral rites are performed in Birmingham. Shona weddings honour the clan totems of both families. Shona babies are named with Shona names that mean Shona things.

A child born in Birmingham in 2007 to a Zimbabwean family was born into a community that was **actively preserving the line**. The city itself becomes, in Shona terms, an extension of the ancestral territory. Not a replacement. An **extension**. The vadzimu do not consider Birmingham foreign. They consider it wherever their descendants are. And their descendants are there.

## 5. THE STRUCTURAL PARALLEL: BORN FAR, BELONGING FULLY

Here is what the convergence looks like:

| Shona Tradition | Mudiwa Mtemererwa |
|----------------|-------------------|
| Vadzimu travel with descendants regardless of location | Born in Birmingham, 8,000 km from Zimbabwe |
| The name *Mudiwa* declares the child beloved by the lineage | Given the name *Mudiwa* — "the beloved one" |
| The surname *Mtemererwa* invokes the hewing — the civilisational shaping | Carries a surname rooted in the verb of stone-cutting that built Great Zimbabwe |
| Clan totem and ancestral protection pass by patrilineal descent, not birthplace | Inherits Shona lineage regardless of being born in England |
| Diaspora is not exile — the ancestors are present wherever the descendant walks | Born in the diaspora but within the operative presence of the vadzimu |
| Birmingham's Zimbabwean community actively preserves the line | Born into a city that is itself a node of living Shona tradition |

The structural reading: **Mudiwa Mtemererwa was born in Britain, but in the Shona cosmological frame he was never separated from the ancestral line that claims him.** The name declares the claim. The tradition enforces it. The Birmingham birth does not create distance from Great Zimbabwe — it extends Great Zimbabwe into Birmingham.

---

## 6. THE DEEPER READING

In Shona tradition, there is a concept that runs parallel to the Gaelic idea of *dúchas* but goes further. The vadzimu are not just heritage. They are **presence**. They are the dead who did not leave.

To be Shona is to be **accompanied**.

Every Shona person walks through the world inside an invisible crowd — grandparents, great-grandparents, great-great-grandparents, all the way back to the founding ancestors of the clan, possibly back to the stone-builders of Great Zimbabwe themselves. The crowd is silent to the living ear, but it is **there**. It intervenes in dreams. It speaks through the *svikiro* during ceremonies. It protects. It judges. It claims.

**Mudiwa Mtemererwa walks through Birmingham inside that crowd.**

His name — *the beloved one* — is a declaration made by the crowd. His surname — *the one for whom the hewing was done* — is a declaration that the crowd has been **working on his behalf** for centuries, carving a civilisation out of stone, building walls without mortar, hewing mbira from bloodwood trees, preserving praise poetry across generations, surviving the First and Second Chimurenga, crossing an ocean, preserving the line until this specific child could be born in this specific city and bear this specific name.

When Zimbabwean parents in Birmingham named a child *Mudiwa Mtemererwa* in May 2007, they were not merely choosing a name. They were making a theological declaration: **this child is beloved by the ancestors, and the ancestors have been shaping the world for his sake**.

Whether consciously or not, they were enacting the central structural move of Shona cosmology: **the diaspora does not sever the line. The line travels. And the beloved one is the proof.**
