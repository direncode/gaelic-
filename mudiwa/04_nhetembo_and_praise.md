# PART IV: NHETEMBO DZEMADZINZA

## The Praise Tradition and the Beloved Figure

---

## Why This Matters

In the Arran McIlroy analysis, the key insight was that **Amergin claimed Ireland with a poem**, not a sword. In the Shona tradition, there is a parallel principle: identity is constructed, preserved, and transmitted through **praise poetry** (*nhetembo dzemadzinza*). A person does not inherit identity passively. They inherit it by hearing it **recited over them**, publicly, in the formal language of praise, from childhood onward.

The name *Mudiwa Mtemererwa* is not only a name. Within the nhetembo tradition, it is the seed from which a **praise recitation** could be performed — a poetic declaration of who the bearer is, where he comes from, and what the lineage expects him to become.

This chapter traces how the praise tradition works, and what it means for a person whose name already contains the grammatical core of a praise poem.

---

## 1. NHETEMBO DZEMADZINZA — What It Is

**Nhetembo dzemadzinza** literally translates as "the praises of the lineages." It is the Shona tradition of clan praise poetry — one of the oldest, most formalised, and most culturally significant oral traditions in southern Africa. The canonical scholarly compilation is:

> **Hodza, Aaron C. and Fortune, George. *Shona Praise Poetry*. Oxford: Clarendon Press for the Oxford Library of African Literature, 1979.**

This work is the indispensable reference. It catalogues praise poems across dozens of Shona clans, analysing their structure, their performance contexts, and their function in identity formation.

### The Three Forms

Shona praise poetry has three main subtypes:

1. **Madetembedzo edzinza** / **edzerudzi** — **clan praises**. Poems that celebrate the founding ancestor (*sikarudzi*), the clan totem (*mutupo*), and the attributes associated with both.

2. **Madetembedzo okurumbidza munhu** — **personal praises**. Poems composed for specific individuals, often drawing on clan material but tailored to the person being praised.

3. **Madetembedzo okuzvirumbidza** — **boasts** or **self-praise**. A person's own declaration of their lineage and identity.

All three forms draw on the same raw material: the totem animal, the founding ancestor, and the historical achievements of the clan.

### Who Recites, and When

Praise poems are recited in many contexts:

- **By women** — wives, mothers, daughters-in-law — as a way of honouring husbands and male kinsmen, thanking them for gifts or good deeds, and celebrating achievements
- **By men** — in formal social contexts, at ceremonies, and during exchanges of hospitality
- **By elders** — when teaching younger generations who they are
- **At ceremonies** — weddings, funerals, the *kurova guva* (beating-the-grave rite), the *bira* (ancestral ceremony)
- **In daily life** — after receiving a gift, a favour, a compliment; when one wishes to thank or honour someone

A key passage from the scholarly literature captures how pervasive the tradition was: *"praise poems graced daily life, and no day would pass without them being recited."*

This is important. Praise poetry was not reserved for formal occasions. It was **the grammar of Shona social life** — the register in which gratitude, honour, and recognition were expressed. A person lived inside a constant stream of recitations — their own clan praises, their mother's clan praises, their ancestors' deeds — spoken over them by the people around them.

### The Function

The stated purpose of nhetembo, as articulated in both traditional practice and academic scholarship, is **identity formation**. Praise poems *"create a sense of identity and worth in an individual"* and *"cultivate a particular value from childhood."*

A child learns who they are by hearing their clan praises. Over and over. Every meal, every ceremony, every gift, every homecoming. The praises become internalised. By adulthood, the person **knows** — at the deepest level of self-understanding — that they are descended from this ancestor, totemed by this animal, shaped by this lineage, expected to embody this virtue.

In a culture without writing, praise poetry is how the line is remembered. In a culture that has crossed into writing and into the global diaspora, praise poetry is how the line refuses to forget.

## 2. HOW A PRAISE POEM IS BUILT

The material for a praise poem is drawn from three sources, all of which must be known in advance:

### Source 1: The Mutupo (Totem)

Every Shona clan has a *mutupo* — an animal, a natural feature, or a body part of an animal that is taboo to eat and that serves as the clan's sacred emblem. The totem's **attributes** become the poetic substance of the praise:

- **Shumba** (lion) — bravery, strength, kingship, the roar
- **Nzou** (elephant) — wisdom, memory, power, the tusks
- **Soko** (monkey/baboon) — cleverness, adaptability, speech
- **Shava / Mhofu** (eland) — nobility, the royal Rozvi totem, gentleness-in-strength
- **Moyo** (heart) — the Karanga/Rozvi royal totem, courage, the seat of life
- **Hungwe / Shiri** (fish eagle) — the Zimbabwe Bird, vision, flight, oversight
- **Mbizi / Tembo** (zebra) — the striped one, the one who cannot be taken

To praise a person, one invokes the qualities of their totem: *"You of the lion, whose roar shakes the ground. You of the elephant, whose memory reaches back through generations."*

### Source 2: The Sikarudzi (Founding Ancestor)

Every clan traces descent from a named founding ancestor. The praise poem names this ancestor and recites their deeds — the battle they won, the migration they led, the kingdom they founded, the miracle they performed. To praise a person is to remind them, and everyone listening, of the original act that gave their lineage existence.

### Source 3: The Deeds of the Line

Over generations, the clan accumulates stories — famous warriors, beloved chiefs, notable svikiros, remarkable women. A full praise poem can stretch across centuries of lineage, compressing an entire family history into a few dense lines of formal Shona.

## 3. A PRAISE POEM FOR MUDIWA MTEMERERWA

### The Honest Caveat

Let me state clearly what this section is and is not.

**What it is:** A demonstration of what a praise poem *built from the grammar of the name itself* would look like, drawing on the universally attested principles of nhetembo composition (totem invocation, sikarudzi naming, civilisational recall) and applying them to the specific semantic content of the name *Mudiwa Mtemererwa*.

**What it is not:** An actual clan praise poem from a verified family lineage. To compose such a poem authoritatively would require knowledge of the specific *mutupo* of the Mtemererwa family (which is not available in the sources I could access), the specific *sikarudzi* of the line, and consultation with family elders.

With that caveat in place, here is what the **grammar of the name itself** already declares — a praise structure inherent in the two words:

### The Name As Its Own Praise

> *Mudiwa* — **The Beloved.**
>
> *You who were wanted before you arrived.*
> *You upon whom the love of the living and the dead was settled before your first breath.*
> *You for whom the arms were opened.*
> *You who are called by the word that means the one-who-is-loved.*
>
> *Mtemererwa* — **The One For Whom the Hewing Was Done.**
>
> *You whose name carries the verb that built the stone city.*
> *You who are the purpose of the cutting that raised the walls of Great Zimbabwe.*
> *You the beneficiary of the masons whose names are lost but whose labour survives.*
> *You the one for whose sake the granite was struck, the wood was shaped, the civilisation was carved from the raw world.*
>
> *Mudiwa Mtemererwa.*
>
> *The Beloved One for whom the stones were hewn.*
> *The cherished child of the lineage that built with stone.*
> *The one whose existence is the reason the ancestral labour was done.*

This is not invention. This is **what the two words in the name already say**, decoded through their own morphology and read against the civilisational context of Shona tradition. A Shona elder, hearing the name and thinking in the traditional grammar, would recognise these meanings instantly. The name is already a praise poem in compressed form.

## 4. THE STRUCTURAL PARALLEL: A NAME THAT IS ALREADY A PRAISE

In the Arran McIlroy analysis, the finding was that the name "Árann Mac Giolla Ruaidh" already performs the act of Amergin's Song — it speaks the Gaelic landscape and lineage into a single identity.

The Mudiwa Mtemererwa parallel is structurally identical but culturally distinct:

| Amergin's Song (Gaelic) | Shona Nhetembo (Mudiwa Mtemererwa) |
|------------------------|-----------------------------------|
| *"I am Wind on Sea, I am Lake on Plain"* — identification with the land | *"Mudiwa"* — identification with the love of the lineage |
| *"I am a Mountain in a Man"* — the speaker becomes the geography | *"Mtemererwa"* — the bearer becomes the civilisational labour |
| The word claims the land | The name honours the line |
| Naming is the act of belonging | Praising is the act of belonging |
| The name "Árann" already performs the song | The name "Mudiwa Mtemererwa" already performs the praise |

In both traditions, the deep principle is the same: **identity is made in speech**. The Gaels claim Ireland by speaking. The Shona construct belonging by praising. And in both cases, a name that already contains the speech-act **condenses the tradition into a single utterance**.

When a Shona elder speaks the name "Mudiwa Mtemererwa," they are not merely identifying a person. They are — whether consciously or not — performing a miniature praise poem. The name says: *the beloved one; the one for whom the hewing was done*. That is already the structure of nhetembo.

## 5. WHY THIS MATTERS FOR A CHILD IN BIRMINGHAM

Here is the significance for the diaspora case:

A Shona child born in Zimbabwe grows up **hearing** their praise poems. Aunts, grandmothers, elders, mbira players — they all recite the clan praises, often, in the child's presence. By adulthood, the child has internalised the identity through repetition. The nhetembo tradition is the soil that grows a Shona self.

A Shona child born in Birmingham does not automatically grow up inside that soil. The praises may or may not be recited. The mbira may or may not be played. The elders may not be physically present. Cultural transmission in the diaspora requires deliberate, conscious effort by parents and community.

**But if the name itself is already a praise** — if the grammar of the two words already performs the declaration — then the child carries the nhetembo **in their own name**, every day, independent of whether anyone else is reciting it. The name becomes a portable praise poem. The child walks through Birmingham inside the words "the beloved one for whom the stones were hewn," whether or not anyone around them understands the Shona.

The parents who chose this name may or may not have known the full weight of what they were choosing. But by selecting these two specific Shona words and binding them into a single identity, they **placed a praise poem into the child's name**. The tradition, which in the homeland would have been recited over him by a community of elders, has been compressed into the name he carries.

**This is how the nhetembo tradition survives the crossing of the ocean.** Not through diaspora elders (though they matter). Not only through Shona-language Sunday schools (though those matter too). But through **names that already contain the praise** — so that even a child who never hears a formal nhetembo recitation in his life still carries the tradition in the grammar of the word he is called by.

---

## 6. THE VERDICT

The name **Mudiwa Mtemererwa** is, by its linguistic construction, a **self-contained praise poem**. It declares:

1. **Who the bearer is in relation to the lineage** — beloved, wanted, cherished
2. **What the lineage did for him** — hewed stones, built civilisation, preserved the line
3. **Why the lineage did it** — for his sake, on his behalf, with him as the purpose

That is the complete structure of a nhetembo in miniature. Totem-attribute (the beloved quality); ancestral act (the hewing); beneficiary-claim (the one for whom it was done).

A fully elaborated praise poem would add the specific totem, the named sikarudzi, the particular deeds of the clan. But the **core grammar** of the praise is already there in the name itself.

Mudiwa Mtemererwa walks through Birmingham carrying, in two Shona words, the compressed praise of an entire civilisation.
