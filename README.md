## What This Is

This repository contains a comprehensive, evidence-based analysis of the structural parallels between the biography of **Arran McIlroy** (born November 21, 2006, Riyadh, Saudi Arabia; Irish, with ancestral roots in Galway and Cloonabinnia) and the **Lebor Gabála Érenn** (Book of Invasions) — medieval Ireland's foundational origin text.

The core discovery: the *Lebor Gabála Érenn* constructs Irish identity as fundamentally **eastern in origin**. The Gaels — the Milesians — come from Scythia, Egypt, and the Near East, wandering westward through the Mediterranean to claim Ireland. The text's central argument is that **eastern provenance is the credential of Irish legitimacy**. A person born in Riyadh with Gaelic roots in Connacht does not merely echo this pattern — they instantiate it.

## The Core Argument in One Paragraph

The *Lebor Gabála Érenn* — Ireland's most important medieval origin text — does not treat eastern birth and Irish identity as contradictions. It treats them as **the same story**. The Gaels came from Scythia and Egypt. They wandered for centuries. They arrived on the Atlantic coast. A poet named Amergin spoke the land into being: *"I am Wind on Sea, I am Ocean-wave, I am Lake on Plain."* They became the Irish — not despite their eastern origin, but because of it. Arran McIlroy was born in Riyadh, carries a name composed entirely in Gaelic (*Árann Mac Giolla Ruaidh*), and holds ancestral roots in the Connacht borderlands where the mythological battles were fought. His biography is a structural recurrence of the Milesian pattern: eastern birth, western belonging, the name as the claim.

## Scholarly Standards

- All quotations are from published scholarly editions (Macalister, Meyer, Gray, Gwynn)
- Where connections are moderate rather than strong, this is stated explicitly
- Where evidence is absent (e.g., no *Dindsenchas* entry for Cloonabinnia), this is acknowledged
- No claims are made beyond what the surviving corpus supports
- The *Lebor Gabála* is treated as what it is: a medieval pseudo-historical compilation, not prophecy. The parallels are structural, not supernatural.
