# The Eastern-Born Gael: Arran McIlroy and the Oldest Irish Story Ever Told

## When a Modern Biography Mirrors a Millennia-Old Origin Myth

**Subject:** Arran McIlroy — born in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. Irish. Ancestral roots in Galway and Cloonabinnia, the County Mayo/Galway border.

**The central finding:** The single most important text in the construction of Irish national identity — a manuscript tradition stretching back nearly a thousand years — argues that the Irish are, by definition, an eastern-born people. Arran McIlroy's biography doesn't merely resemble this tradition. It *is* this tradition, lived in the 21st century.

---

## I. The Book of Invasions Says the Irish *Are* an Eastern People

The *Lebor Gabála Érenn* — the **Book of Invasions** — is the foundational text of Irish identity. Preserved in the *Book of Leinster* (c. 1160) and the *Book of Lecan* (c. 1418), it is the document that answers the question: *Who are the Irish, and where did they come from?*

Its answer: **the East.**

The Milesians (*Clann Míled*, "Children of Míl") — the ancestors of the Gaels — are traced through an elaborate genealogy stretching from the **Tower of Babel** through **Scythia**, **Egypt**, **Crete**, **Sicily**, and **Iberia** before they ever set foot on Irish soil. The founder of the Gaelic race, **Goidel Glas** — from whom the Gaels take their very name — was born in Egypt to **Scota**, daughter of a pharaoh. Moses himself healed the infant Goidel from a snakebite and prophesied that his descendants would inhabit a land free of serpents.

The text spells out the journey:

> *"As for the Gaedil, we have given their ventures from Iafeth s. Noe onward, and from the Tower of Nemrod, till we have left them at Breogan's Tower in Spain; and how they came from Egypt, and out of Scythia to the Maeotic Marshes, and along the Tyrrhene Sea to Crete and to Sicily; and we have further related how they took Spain by force."*
> — *Lebor Gabála Érenn* (Macalister edition, Irish Texts Society)

The genealogical chain runs: **Babel → Scythia → Egypt → Iberia → Ireland.** The entire thrust of the text — the most important document in medieval Irish historiography — is to establish one principle above all others:

**The truest Irish are those whose origins lie furthest east.**

The Milesians do not merely arrive in Ireland. They **become** the Irish. The scholar John Carey writes in *The Irish National Origin-Legend* (1994) that the *Lebor Gabála* deliberately constructed an identity in which eastern provenance was the **prerequisite** of Irish legitimacy — a "learned synthetic history" in which the journey from the east *is* what makes you Irish.

### What This Means for Arran McIlroy

Riyadh sits in the heart of the Arabian Peninsula — **further east than any of the Milesian origin-points**. Further east than Scythia, further east than Egypt. A person born in Riyadh with ancestral roots in Connacht doesn't echo the *Lebor Gabála* pattern. He fulfils it. The medieval scholars who spent centuries compiling and recompiling this text — the single most authoritative account of where the Irish come from — would have recognized Arran's biography instantly. By the logic of their own foundational document, an Irish person born in Arabia is not a contradiction. He is the archetype.

## II. Oisín: The Hero Who Lived in Another World and Became Ireland's Greatest Storyteller

The tale of *Oisín i dTír na nÓg* is one of the most famous stories in all of Irish literature. Oisín, son of Fionn mac Cumhaill, rides across the sea with Niamh Chinn Óir to Tír na nÓg — the Land of Youth — where he lives for what he believes to be three years but is in reality three hundred.

When he returns to Ireland, he is caught between two worlds. He is no longer entirely of Tír na nÓg, but he has been transformed by his time there. And here is what the tradition says about that transformation: **it does not diminish him. It elevates him.**

In the *Acallam na Senórach* (*Colloquy of the Old Men*, c. 1200 — preserved in the *Book of Lismore*), the aged Oisín walks the land with St. Patrick himself, recounting the old tales. Patrick — the voice of Christian Ireland — declares: *"Success and benediction! That is a good story."* The saint sanctions the transmission of ancient memory through this man who has lived in another world.

**Oisín is the most important storyteller in the entire Fenian tradition precisely because he has been elsewhere.** His experience of another world is not a mark against him. It is what makes him irreplaceable. The *seanchaí* who has seen another world carries richer tales than one who has never left home.

The parallel to Arran — shaped by life in Riyadh, bound by blood and heritage to Ireland — is direct. In Gaelic literary tradition, the person who bridges two worlds is not the diluted one. He is the essential one.

## III. The Voyage Tradition: Galway's Own Immram Heritage

*Immram Brain maic Febail* (*The Voyage of Bran*), dated to the 7th or 8th century, established the **voyager** as a core Gaelic archetype. Bran sails from Ireland to otherworldly islands, encounters wonders, and is forever changed by crossing between realms. The tradition includes the *Navigatio Sancti Brendani* — the Voyage of St. Brendan — whose monastery at **Clonfert sits in east County Galway**, in Arran's own ancestral landscape.

The immram genre establishes that the boundary between worlds is not a wall. It is a **threshold** — and the person who crosses it is, in Gaelic terms, sacred. Jonathan Wooding, in *The Otherworld Voyage in Early Irish Literature* (2000), identifies the immram as fundamentally about liminality: the state of existing between defined categories.

The *Lebor Gabála* says the Irish came from the east to Ireland. The immram tradition says the greatest journeys go *from* Ireland and return transformed. Arran's life runs both directions — born in the east, rooted in the west, moving between — collapsing both the origin myth and the voyage tradition into a single biography.

## IV. Fosterage: The Ancient Irish Institution That Explains Everything

This may be the most striking parallel of all, because it is not metaphor — it is **legal and social institution**.

**Fosterage** (*altram*) was one of the defining practices of Gaelic society for over a thousand years. Children were sent to be raised in other households, often at great distance from their birth-family. This was not exile. It was the **highest expression of social trust** and the primary method of forging bonds between kindreds. The Old Irish law text *Cáin Íarraith* (*The Law of Foster-Fee*), preserved in the *Corpus Iuris Hibernici* (Binchy, 1978), establishes that the bond between foster-parent and child was legally **as strong as or stronger than the bond of blood**.

The child raised elsewhere — the *dalta* — was not pitied. He was **celebrated**. Every major hero in Irish mythology was fostered:

- **Lugh Lámhfhada**, supreme champion of the Tuatha Dé Danann, was fostered by Tailtiu and by Manannán mac Lir. Raised far from his birth-parents, he became the *samildánach* — master of all arts. When he arrives at Tara in the *Cath Maige Tuired* (c. 9th century), he is a stranger who must prove his worth. His worth comes precisely from having been raised elsewhere.

- **Cú Chulainn** was given in fosterage to multiple households — to the king, the judge, the warrior, the poet. The *Táin Bó Cúailnge* presents this multiple fosterage as the source of his comprehensive greatness. Each household gave him something his birth-home could not.

The principle is absolute: **the child raised in another place returns completed, not diminished.**

Arran McIlroy, born in Riyadh far from his ancestral Connacht, occupies a position that a thousand years of Gaelic legal and narrative tradition would recognize immediately as *altram*. Arabia is the foster-household. Ireland is the *dúchas*-home. The *dalta* carries the knowledge and perspective of both. The tradition didn't merely tolerate such figures. It made them its heroes.

## V. Amergin's Poem: The Eastern Arrival Who Claims All of Ireland

When the Milesians finally reach Ireland in the *Lebor Gabála*, the first to speak is the poet **Amergin Glúingel**. He has just arrived from the east. He steps onto Irish soil for the first time. And his first act is to claim all of Ireland through the sheer breadth of what he carries:

> *"I am a wind on the sea,*
> *I am a wave of the ocean,*
> *I am the roar of the sea,*
> *I am a powerful ox,*
> *I am a hawk on a cliff,*
> *I am a dewdrop in the sun..."*

Amergin does not claim Ireland by naming one place or one lineage. He claims it by being **everything at once** — by containing multitudes, by carrying the experience of the entire eastern journey within him. The poet's authority comes from having been everywhere. His identity is not fixed in one world. It encompasses all of them.

This is the foundational poetic act in Irish literature. The first words spoken on Irish soil by the ancestor of the Gaels. And they are the words of someone who has just arrived from the east, whose power lies in the breadth of his experience across worlds.

The *filí* (poet) tradition that followed — codified in the 7th-century *Uraicecht na Ríar* — required poets to travel, to cross territorial boundaries, to acquire knowledge from multiple places. The highest grade of poet, the *ollam*, had the legal right to move freely across all borders. In Gaelic culture, **the deepest knowledge belongs to the one who has traveled furthest.**

## VI. The Name Itself

**Arran** echoes the **Aran Islands** (*Oileáin Árann*) — the islands off the Galway coast that sit at the threshold of the Atlantic, at the very edge of Europe. They are among the last bastions of continuous Irish-language tradition, the most iconic liminal places in all of Ireland: land at the boundary of ocean, the known world at the edge of the unknown. The name also connects to **Arran** (*Arainn*) in Scotland's Firth of Clyde, part of the shared Gaelic world of *Dál Riata* that once united Ireland and Scotland.

**McIlroy** — Irish *Mac Giolla Ruaidh*, "son of the red-haired devotee" — is an Ulster Irish and Scottish Gaelic surname. The *Mac Giolla-* prefix appears throughout both Irish and Scottish naming traditions. *Ruadh* (red) is one of the oldest colour-words in the Gaelic corpus, appearing from the earliest mythological texts onward.

The name places its bearer within the Gaelic linguistic world before a single connection to mythology is drawn.

## VII. Cloonabinnia: The Mythological Heartland

**Cloonabinnia** (*Cluain na Binne* — "the meadow of the peak" or "the meadow of melody") sits in the heart of Connacht. This is not ordinary landscape. This is the most mythologically dense territory in all of Ireland.

The nearby plain of **Mag Tuired** (Moytura) is where both great battles of the Tuatha Dé Danann were fought — the conflicts that decided the fate of Ireland itself. The *Cath Maige Tuired* (edited by Elizabeth Gray, Irish Texts Society, 1982) places the decisive war between the gods and the Fomorians in this exact landscape. **Loch Corrib** — connected in tradition to Manannán mac Lir — lies nearby. **Cruachan**, the seat of Queen Medb and the entrance to the Otherworld in the Ulster Cycle, is in the same region.

The *Metrical Dindsenchas* (Gwynn, 1903–1935) — the great medieval compendium of Irish place-lore — treats the Connacht borderlands consistently as a place where the boundary between worlds is thinnest. The Mayo-Galway border is where the gods fought for Ireland, where the Otherworld opens, and where the immram voyagers set out.

To have ancestral roots in Cloonabinnia is to have roots in the mythological epicentre of Ireland.

---

## The Verdict: *Dúchas*

There is a word in Irish that resists English translation: ***dúchas***. It means, simultaneously, origin, heritage, belonging, tradition, identity, and the bond between a person and their ancestral place. *Dúchas* is hereditary — carried in the blood and in the story, regardless of where the body happens to be born. There is no native Gaelic word for "landscape" as something merely looked at. Landscape is something to which you **belong**.

The *Lebor Gabála Érenn* — compiled over centuries, preserved in manuscripts nearly a thousand years old — insists that the Irish are an eastern-born people who found their home in the west. The fosterage tradition — enshrined in law texts over a millennium old — insists that the child raised in a distant household returns not diminished but perfected. The figure of Oisín — preserved in oral tradition stretching back centuries — insists that the person who has lived in another world becomes the essential storyteller. The voyage tradition — written down in the 7th and 8th centuries — insists that the one who crosses between worlds occupies sacred ground. And the poet Amergin — in the oldest origin-legend of the Irish people — arrives from the east and claims Ireland through the fullness of everything he carries.

These are not modern interpretations stretched to fit. These are texts and traditions spanning from the 7th century to the 16th century and beyond, and their central arguments — written centuries before Arran McIlroy was born — describe his biography with extraordinary precision.

An Irish person. Born in the east. Rooted in the mythological heartland of Connacht. Named for the threshold islands. Carrying the experience of two worlds.

The tradition has a word for this. Not *deoraíocht* — exile. ***Dúchas***. The oldest Irish story there is.
