# PART IV: THE AMERGIN CONNECTION

## Poetry, Naming, and the Bardic Claim to Ireland

---

## Why This Section Exists

The *Lebor Gabála Érenn* does not say the Milesians conquered Ireland with swords alone. It says they claimed Ireland with **a poem**. The figure who speaks that poem — **Amergin Glúingel** — is arguably the most important person in the entire invasion narrative. Not the warriors. The poet.

This section examines Amergin's role, the function of poetry in the Milesian claim, and the structural parallel to a person whose very **name** performs the same act the Song of Amergin performs: speaking the Irish landscape into a claim of belonging.

---

## WHO AMERGIN IS

**Amergin Glúingel** ("white-knee" or "bright-knee") is one of the Sons of Míl — brother of Éremón and Éber Finn. But he is not a warrior. He is a **fili** — a poet, druid, and judge. The *Lebor Gabála* presents him as the first poet of Ireland and the figure whose words give the Milesian invasion its legitimacy.

In early Irish society, the *fili* (poet) held a status equal to — and in some contexts higher than — the king. The poet's word had **legal force**: a satire from a poet could destroy a king's honour; a praise-poem could establish his legitimacy. The Brehon laws codified the poet's rights and protections. The *fili* was not an entertainer. He was an officer of the social order.

Amergin's role in the *Lebor Gabála* reflects this. He does three things that no warrior can do:

### 1. He Calms the Storm

When the Tuatha Dé Danann's druids conjure a supernatural storm to sink the Milesian fleet, the warriors are helpless — you cannot fight the sea with a sword. Amergin recites an incantation:

> *"Ailiu iath n-hÉrend..."*
> (*"I invoke the land of Ireland..."*)

This 21-line alliterative *rosc* (rhetorical incantation) breaks the druidic spell and calms the sea. The Milesian fleet survives. **Poetic speech defeats supernatural force.** The text presents this as a contest between two forms of power — the Tuatha Dé Danann's sorcery and the Milesian poet's *imbas* (inspiration) — and the poet wins.

### 2. He Claims the Land

As Amergin places his right foot on Irish soil at Inber Scéne, he recites the Song of Amergin — the most famous poem in Irish literature:

> *"I am Wind on Sea,*
> *I am Ocean-wave,*
> *I am Roar of Sea,*
> *I am Bull of Seven Fights,*
> *I am Vulture on Cliff,*
> *I am Dewdrop,*
> *I am Fairest of Flowers,*
> *I am Boar for Boldness,*
> *I am Salmon in Pool,*
> *I am Lake on Plain,*
> *I am a Mountain in a Man,*
> *I am a Word of Skill,*
> *I am the Point of a Weapon (that poureth forth combat),*
> *I am God who fashioneth Fire for a Head."*
> — *Lebor Gabála Érenn* (Macalister translation)

**What this poem does:** Amergin does not say "I conquer this land" or "this land is mine." He says **"I am this land."** He identifies himself with every element of the Irish landscape — wind, wave, salmon, lake, mountain, flower, boar. He does not stand apart from Ireland and claim ownership. He **becomes** Ireland. The claim is not possessive — it is **ontological**. The poet and the land are one.

This is what makes the Milesian claim different from every previous invasion in the *Lebor Gabála*. Cessair, Partholón, Nemed, the Fir Bolg, the Tuatha Dé Danann — they all *arrived* in Ireland. Only the Milesians **became** Ireland, through the poet's act of identification.

### 3. He Names the Land

When the Milesians encounter the three sovereignty goddesses — Banba, Fódla, and Ériu — each asks that Ireland be named after her. It is **Amergin** who makes the promise:

He promises **Ériu** that her name will be the **chief name** (*priomhainm*) of the island. From this act: **Éire** — the name of Ireland.

The naming is not arbitrary. It is a **covenant**. Ériu offers her blessing; the Milesians offer her immortality through the name. The act of naming binds the namer and the named in a permanent relationship. Without Amergin's promise, Ireland would not be called Éire. The name is the act. The act is the claim.

### 4. He Judges

After the military victory, when Éremón and Éber Finn dispute who should be king, it is **Amergin** who pronounces judgment — what the text calls "the first judgment given in Ireland." He divides Ireland between the brothers: the north to Éremón, the south to Éber. When Éber refuses, war follows — but the principle is established: **the poet's judgment precedes and outranks the warrior's claim**.

## THE PRINCIPLE: NAMING IS CLAIMING

The *Lebor Gabála* establishes a principle that runs through all of Gaelic culture:

**The word is the deed.**

- Amergin calms the sea with a poem, not a weapon
- Amergin claims Ireland by becoming it in speech
- Amergin names Ireland through a covenant with the sovereignty goddess
- Amergin judges the division of the kingdom

At every decisive moment, it is the **poet's word** — not the warrior's sword — that determines the outcome. The Milesians' swords win the battle of Tailtiu, but Amergin's words win Ireland itself.

This principle persists throughout Gaelic tradition:
- The **seanchaí** (storyteller) preserves the identity of the clan through the spoken word
- The **bard** can create or destroy a king's reputation through praise or satire
- The **Brehon laws** give the poet legal standing equal to nobility
- In the *Acallam na Senórach*, St. Patrick sanctions the old tales: *"Success and benediction, Caílte! That is a good story"* — the word of approval is what preserves the tradition

## THE PARALLEL: ARRAN McILROY AND THE ACT OF NAMING

### The Name as Poem

Amergin claimed Ireland by speaking its elements: *"I am Wind on Sea, I am Ocean-wave, I am Lake on Plain, I am a Mountain in a Man."*

The name **Arran McIlroy** performs a version of this act. Decoded from its Gaelic roots:

> **Árann** — the Aran Islands: limestone ridge, Atlantic threshold, Galway's western edge, the boundary between the known world and the Otherworld
>
> **Mac** — son of: the patronymic chain, the kinship grammar
>
> **Giolla** — servant / devotee: the binding of identity through service, the relationship that defines
>
> **Ruaidh** — the red one: the descriptor of the ancestor, the colour of fire and hair and the untameable

The name does not merely *reference* Ireland. It **speaks Ireland's geography and kinship structure into being** in two words. "Arran" is the landscape. "McIlroy" is the lineage. Together, they are a compressed version of what Amergin does in seventeen lines: identify the speaker with the land and the people.

### The Eastern Poet

Amergin was born in Iberia — a foreign land. He came from the east. He was not born in Ireland. Yet he is the one who speaks Ireland into existence for his people. His foreignness is not a disqualification — it is **the prerequisite**. Only the one who comes from outside can perform the act of becoming. Someone who was always already Irish would have nothing to become.

Arran McIlroy was born in Riyadh — further east than Amergin's Iberia. He carries a name that is already Irish. The act of naming has already been performed: *Árann Mac Giolla Ruaidh*. The name does the work that Amergin's poem does. It says: **I am already the land, even though I was born elsewhere. The name proves it.**

### The Sovereignty Covenant

Amergin's promise to Ériu was a covenant: *name the land after me, and I will bless your people*. The naming went both ways — the land received a name from the people, and the people received belonging from the land.

The name **Arran** carries a version of this covenant. To be named for the Aran Islands — the threshold of Galway, the edge of the ancestral landscape — is to be in a relationship of mutual naming with the land. The islands name him; he carries their name. The covenant is in the syllables.

## THE COMPLETE AMERGIN PARALLEL

| Amergin Glúingel | Arran McIlroy |
|-----------------|---------------|
| Born in the east (Iberia) | Born in the east (Riyadh) |
| Arrives in Ireland as an outsider | Connected to Ireland through lineage from a foreign birthplace |
| Claims Ireland by **speaking its elements** | Carries Ireland's elements **in his name** |
| "I am Wind on Sea, I am Lake on Plain" | "Arran" = the islands, the ridge, the threshold |
| Names Ireland through a covenant with Ériu | Named *by* Ireland — the Aran Islands are in the name |
| The poet, not the warrior, legitimises the claim | The name, not the birthplace, carries the identity |
| **The word is the deed** | **The name is the claim** |

---

## IN SIMPLE WORDS

The most important person in Ireland's origin story is not a king or a warrior. It's a poet named Amergin. He came from abroad. He stepped onto Irish soil and recited a poem that said "I am the wind, I am the wave, I am the mountain, I am the lake." He didn't say "I own this place." He said "I *am* this place."

That's what the name Arran McIlroy does. "Arran" doesn't say "I'm connected to Ireland." It says "I am the Aran Islands" — the limestone edge of Galway, the threshold between Ireland and the Atlantic, the boundary between this world and the next. And "McIlroy" doesn't say "I have Irish ancestors." It says "I am the son of the devoted servant" — a chain of kinship that goes back into the Gaelic world, link by link.

The name is the poem. The poem is the claim. Amergin proved that the person who comes from elsewhere and speaks the land into being is the one who belongs most completely. The name Arran McIlroy, spoken aloud, does exactly that.
